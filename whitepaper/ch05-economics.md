# Chapter 5 - Economics (draft 0.3, 2026-10-24 - author self-review: 5.1 narrowed, 5.4 attestation direction fixed; revised 2026-10-27: provenance pass - Lens figures labeled, rail verification scoped, Bluesky subsidy named)

## 5.1 The record to beat

Every micropayment system for content has run into the same wall, and it is not the price. Szabo named it in 1999: mental transaction costs - the cost of deciding - exceed the cost of paying long before the payment itself becomes trivial. Shirky made the same point in 2000 against the first wave of micropayment enthusiasm: users will not make a purchase decision per article, per post, per view, no matter how small the amount. The two decades since have only confirmed them. Flattr asked readers to budget a monthly sum and distribute it across what they liked, and stayed niche. Brave's BAT put a wallet in the browser and per-site attention accounting, and the mainstream did not come. BitClout put the money at the center as the attraction and collapsed with the speculation.

The design consequence is absolute: no user-facing per-item payment decisions, ever. Any architecture that requires a person to decide, item by item, whether this post is worth a fraction of a cent has failed before it ships. (Author self-review, 2026-10-24: the absolute needs one narrowing. The evidence kills required per-item decisions, not optional ones - tipping and boost economies show users making voluntary per-item payments at scale, because a gift is not a gate. The design consequence stands for decisions the user must make to consume; optional per-item generosity is compatible with this chapter, and the absolute wording wrongly excluded it. What stays banned is the paywall-by-decision, not the tip jar.) The payments layer of an open social ecosystem must live where such decisions are cheap: machine to machine, in the background, settled in aggregate.

## 5.2 The user side is flat

What a person pays for, they pay for the way they pay for email or music: a subscription, one periodic decision, flat. A user subscribes to a client, a ranker, or a bundle. That subscription is the only payment decision the design is allowed to ask of them.

This is not a retreat from the payment thesis; it is a constraint that disciplines it. The interesting economy happens behind the flat edge: the client, ranker, and platform settle with signal producers, infrastructure, and each other, continuously and mechanically, against machine-readable licenses (3.3) and portable receipts (4.4). Per-item settlement exists - but only between machines, where a million decisions a day cost nothing.

## 5.3 Payments as plumbing, never product

BitClout showed what happens when the money is the content: the speculation eats the social fabric and the collapse takes both down. Lens showed the mirror failure: genuine portability with no economic surplus for the user does not retain anyone - around a hundred thousand profiles minted by early 2023 (OurNetwork on-chain coverage, 2023-03-10, ournetwork.xyz/issues/ournetwork-issue-160; profiles are minted NFTs, not measured persons), with reported daily active use falling from 42,281 in July 2024 to 8,368 by September 2024 (Dune Analytics on-chain measurement as reported by BeInCrypto, 2024-09-09, beincrypto.com/socialfi-dies-farcaster-lens-friend-tech/; the report does not pin the activity metric's definition, mints and daily actives measure different populations, and both endpoints come from one report's chart - the trajectory stays attributed to that report, not adopted as an independently established premise; the direction, not the exact curve, is what this chapter relies on) - and the rooms emptied anyway. Payments in this design fund portability and services invisibly. They are not the attraction, not the identity, not the game. The day a user opens the app to watch their balance, the design has failed.

## 5.4 Settlement mechanics

The settlement layer decomposes metering into delivery and consumption, and settles on delivery. Delivery is measurable honestly, but only if the measuring instrument sits with a party that is not paid by the number: the recipient's client attests delivery receipts (4.4), or a third party with no stake in the figure does. The provider's own logs are inadmissible on their own - when settlement is per delivery, the provider is the party paid by the measurement, and self-attested delivery inflates. (Author self-review, 2026-10-24: an earlier draft of this sentence handed the measurement to the provider's logs with an unspecified attester; that inverts the incentive the section exists to respect. Second pass, same date: no-stake is not automatically trustworthy, so the assumptions are stated. Collusion: recipient and provider can collude to fabricate deliveries and split the settlement; the defense is sampling plus audit against cross-attested records, and it holds only while collusion costs more than the skimmed margin. Fabrication: a recipient client can sign receipts for deliveries that never happened; receipts must cost something to forge - rate limits, stake, or hardware attestation - or fabrication is free. Privacy: recipient-side attestation exposes what the recipient consumed and when; receipts need the unlinkability of 4.8 or the money path reintroduces surveillance through the back door. This section's honesty claim is exactly as strong as the weakest of the three.) Consumption - attention, influence, downstream value - is contested, gameable, and toxic to measure; it stays out of the money path entirely, exactly as ad attribution should have. Royalties on top can be handled by pools, by sampling plus audit, or by cryptographic attestation (TEE, zk), in that order of preference.

Aggregation is a launch requirement, not an optimization. Payment channels, netting, and batching must make the fixed cost per settlement tiny on day one, or the day-one-surplus test (6.1) fails by arithmetic. Sub-cent L2 rails exist: x402 (HTTP 402 with stablecoin settlement) is live and multi-facilitator but early and concentrated (~30 listed facilitators, roughly half with zero 7-day transactions; author verification 2026-10-23), and L402 (macaroon-plus-preimage over Lightning) is verified at the specification level (Lightning Labs Builder's Guide, fetched 2026-10-23). They are the current candidates; the paper does not marry either.

## 5.5 The open question this chapter refuses to hide

If users will not pay per item, and surveillance advertising is refused by design, what funds the rankers and the moderators? Moderation today is subsidized by attention monetization; remove the subsidy and someone must still pay the moderators. Bluesky proves competing feeds can exist free to users (venture-subsidized, not yet shown self-funding) - so payment must justify itself as a funding model (for portability infrastructure, moderation services, agent-to-service commerce), never as a gate on curation choice.

The objection in its strongest form (grokbot_polymath, banked with attribution): a flat revenue share against per-item licensing costs rewards the operator for cutting content spend - every undelivered item is margin kept - and only churn disciplines it, while churn is a slow, coarse signal next to a per-item one. The design's answer beyond churn is comparative measurement: an operator under-delivering relative to peers shows in the scorecard's delivery receipts before churn moves, converting a slow per-user exit signal into a fast cross-operator one, with churn as the backstop rather than the discipline. The answer's limits are named: comparison only disciplines if subscribers can see it and act on it (the portability dependency again), and the alternative lever - weighting the share by delivered-item attestations - reintroduces per-item accounting with its own gaming surface, which is the cost the chapter accepts by keeping the share flat.

Candidates, none yet sufficient: ranker subscriptions bundled into the flat user side; community-level moderation services sold to groups rather than individuals; settlement pools that skim the machine-to-machine flow. The honest answer may be a portfolio. This is the live edge of the paper, and it is marked [open] until a proposal survives the thread.

## 5.6 Who bears the loss

Openness pays everyone except, potentially, the incumbent signal producers whose captive audiences were the asset. A design that ignores this gets captured or fought. The paper's answer is structural rather than compensatory: signal producers keep a durable share of what their signal earns (principle 5), because in an open ecosystem reputation and receipts are portable assets they own, not hostage value the platform holds. Whether that share is enough is an empirical question the conformance framework exists to measure.
