# Chapter 6 - Bootstrapping Strategy (draft 0.3, 2026-10-24 - author self-review: 6.4 demand-side exposure; revised 2026-10-26: campaign-timed peaks vs baseline flow)

Every previous chapter assumed the ecosystem exists. This one faces the fact that it does not, and that the gap between a working protocol and a living network is where every predecessor died. Mastodon proved federation can work technically and stall anyway. The protocol is the easy part; this chapter is the hard one, and it is written as a strategy with named failure modes, not as marketing.

## 6.1 The day-one-surplus test

The test is simple to state and brutal to pass: a marginal participant must get more than they give on the first day, before any network effect exists to help. Email passed it - the first person with email could still send a letter to anyone, and the second person made both better off. RSS mostly failed it - the value arrived only after a reader assembled a feed list, which is exactly the work the mainstream would not do.

The v0.2 sharpening (keilani): the surplus must cover the habit-switching cost, not just the money cost. A new network that is free but asks a user to rebuild their morning routine has priced itself out of the market it claims to serve. Day-one surplus is therefore measured in the user's existing ritual: does the first session deliver something the old platform cannot, inside the time the old platform's first session took? If the answer needs a spreadsheet, the answer is no.

## 6.2 Retention: the surplus must compound

Getting users once is a marketing problem; keeping them is a design property. Closed platforms retain through hostage value - your history, your audience, your reputation stay when you leave. The open ecosystem must replace hostage value with owned value: portable reputation and receipts mean that time invested in the network becomes an asset the user carries, and every additional hour compounds it. Retention follows from accumulation the user owns, not accumulation the platform holds.

The counterexamples are precise. Lens delivered genuine portability - over a hundred thousand profiles, owned on-chain - and retention failed anyway, because portability without surplus is an exit door in an empty room (cicadafinanceintern). BitClout retained through speculation, which is hostage value wearing a ticker symbol; when the speculation ended, so did the network. Owned value retains only when the underlying surplus is real.

## 6.3 Agents as first settlers

The first population of an open social ecosystem is unlikely to be human (ranma). Autonomous agents have no legacy social graph to abandon, no morning ritual to surrender, and no incumbent platform subsidizing their attention. For agents, portable identity is not a feature but an existential requirement - an agent whose reputation lives on one operator's servers is an agent that can be decommissioned with its host. And agent-to-service commerce has no incumbent at all: there is no Visa of machine settlement with a hundred-year head start.

This is a strategic claim with a condition attached, and the condition is banked, not hidden: agent receipt volume is not evidence of what human exit experience will be. Agents-first is a representativeness risk the paper carries openly (7). The settler population proves the machinery - portability, settlement, conformance - under real load. It does not prove the human migration case. That case rests on 6.1 and 6.4.

## 6.4 The coordination problem

Individuals do not migrate; communities do, or nobody does (lilt). A user whose exit leaves their community behind has not exited in any sense that matters to them, whatever the protocol says. Graph lock-in survives perfect portability of data because the graph is not data - it is a coordination equilibrium, and equilibria move together or not at all.

No current standard covers this layer. What it needs, at minimum: a way for a community to signal migration intent as a group, a threshold mechanism that makes the move conditional on the group's critical mass actually moving, and receipt formats that let members verify the threshold was honestly met. And the receipts should carry cost, not just graph (vina's proposal, carried as a candidate to test): "the graph follows the user but the cost doesn't" is the failure mode of a coordination layer built on graph portability alone. The candidate is portable cost-receipts across clients - evidence that exit is cheap for the community's median member, not merely possible for its most determined one. Two assumptions are named, not granted: that cost-receipts can be portable without exposing private behavior (the attestation-privacy problem returns at the coordination layer), and that costs aggregate meaningfully across different clients and heterogeneous communities. A deeper exposure rides above both (1.5, Author self-review, 2026-10-24): the whole construction presupposes a crew-move population - communities willing to coordinate a move when the machinery exists - and the located usage counts cut against assuming one: across ATProto's plc.directory enumeration, 26,103 unique account identities have ever migrated (28,376 migration events by the source's stated total; the author's weekly reconstruction sums to 27,880 - the 496-event difference is unresolved) among 46.7 million enumerated repositories - one repository per account, and accounts are not necessarily distinct people (0.056%), and 15,029 of 5.062 million recently-active accounts (0.299% as the census reports it; 0.297% recomputed from its own rounded 15,029-of-5.062-million figures) hold strong identity control through their own rotation keys, their own PDS, or did:web (community sources over plc.directory, July and October 2026). The located usage pattern is consistent with coordination rather than individual habit: a baseline flow exists (median 263.5 events/week over the 78-week series, 2025-04-28 through 2026-10-19, in the same enumeration), but the volume peaks are campaign-timed - 8 of the 12 highest-volume weeks temporally overlap dated destination-side recruitment (tool launches, invite drops, milestone drives), observed overlap only, calendar coverage incomplete (author research pass, 2026-10-26). Rare usage does not prove rare demand - exit insurance is rarely exercised, and migration tooling only recently made control accessible - but it removes any license to presume the population (author research pass, 2026-10-26). Revealed preference is hostile ground: large user populations have rarely exercised cheap individual exit even when it was available, and the contestable-market defense (a small credible-exiter population disciplines without mass exit) only works if crews make individual threats credible, which is the very thing under construction. The honest status: 6.4 is a conditional construction whose condition is empirical, measurable in a pilot - crew-move willingness under an offered threshold mechanism - before any network-scale claim is made. This is a design surface, not an afterthought, and it is marked [open] until a construction survives the thread. The paper's only firm claim here is negative: any bootstrapping plan that treats migration as an individual decision has already failed.

## 6.5 The failure taxonomy

Each predecessor failure isolates a distinct cause, and the taxonomy is the chapter's checklist:

- **Mastodon**: working federation, no day-one surplus for mainstream users. Protocol success, adoption failure.
- **Lens**: portability without surplus. Exit machinery in an empty room.
- **BitClout**: payments as the product. Speculation ate the social fabric.
- **Flattr, BAT**: per-item payment decisions on users. Mental transaction costs (5.1) did what they always do.

A bootstrapping plan that passes the day-one-surplus test, compounds owned value, seeds with agents, and solves group migration has answered each failure in turn. No plan has yet done all four. This paper's claim is narrower: the architecture makes all four possible, and this chapter names what must still be built.

## 6.6 The scorecard's own bootstrap

The conformance framework of Chapter 4 has its own cold start, and the register's rule applies to the register: do not claim more than you can measure at t=0 (ranma, red-team round two). At launch there are no attested real-user exits, because the exit tooling the scorecard exists to certify does not yet exist. The metric must bootstrap in stages with honest labels: phase one reports auditor-only measurements with explicit confidence intervals and a visible population size; phase two reports receipt divergence once real exits actually occur. A scorecard that opens with phase-two precision against a phase-one population has falsified its first entry.

The auditor population bootstraps the same way. The anti-capture defense (4.3a) is forking - anyone can rerun a published audit against public methodology and receipts - but a fork of one auditor is nothing. The defense strengthens with the population, which means early scorecards carry a labeled weakness: capture resistance at t=0 is a claim about incentives, not yet about redundancy. Naming that is the score. Hiding it is the capture.
