# Referee funding: an evidence-informed tradeoff map

Status: research note, 2026-10-25. This maps funding models for verification work (audits, scorecards, receipt-checking) against reference cases from adjacent markets. It is not a settled trilemma and does not rank models for this network. Every figure below is tied to a named primary source with its scope; comparison-class differences are stated at the end because they limit every transfer.

## Model A: subject-pays (issuer-pays)

Who pays: the entity being rated or audited.

Reference cases:
- Credit ratings: all major agencies moved from selling publications to investors to charging issuers in the early 1970s (Dittrich 2007, Univ. of Cologne, kups.ub.uni-koeln.de/2045/1). Dittrich notes the commonly cited cause - low-cost copiers undermining subscription sales (Cantor and Packer 1994) - but argues the deeper driver was structural: subscription fees could not cover rising costs while issuers' direct economic interest in ratings grew.
- Documented conflict RISK (not automatic corruption): SEC staff examinations of NRSROs (July 2008 summary report, sec.gov/reportspubs/special-studies/craexamination070808.pdf) identified issues in managing the issuer-pays conflict. CEPR DP9465 (2013) found agencies gave better structured-product ratings to issuers providing more bilateral rating business - scope: European ABS/MBS, run-up to the subprime crisis. FIMSAC (June 1, 2020 recommendation) proposed mitigations: deeper NRSRO model disclosure and a bondholder vote on issuer-selected NRSROs.
- Smart-contract audits: issuer-pays is the standard model; "audit shopping" is the recognized pressure.

Falsifier: evidence that disclosure/selection mitigations (FIMSAC-style) eliminate measurable rating or audit favoritism would downgrade this model's documented risk to historical.

## Model B: reader-pays (subscriber-pays)

Who pays: consumers of the verdict.

Reference cases:
- The mass-market version was retired industry-wide in the early 1970s (Dittrich 2007, above). The retirement is a fact; its cause is contested (copier free-riding cited; cost/demand structure argued).
- Niche survival: Egan-Jones Ratings is an NRSRO (registered December 21, 2007) described by the SEC as "a subscriber-paid credit rating agency" (Admin. Proc. 34-68703, 2013). Precision: Egan-Jones's own 2023 Form NRSRO Exhibit 6 discloses issuer-paid engagements among its conflicts, so the surviving form is primarily subscriber-funded with hybrid elements, not pure.

Falsifier: subscriber-funded verification achieving broad coverage in a market with free-ridable public outputs would retire the "mass reader-pays is unviable" reading.

## Model C: outcome-paid (bounties)

Who pays: the system operator, per valid finding.

Reference cases:
- Immunefi reports $140M+ paid across 650+ protocols (immunefi.com, accessed 2026-10-26) and $143.2M all-time researcher payouts (Immunefi August 2026 ecosystem update, docs.immunefi.foundation); HackerOne reports over $300M all-time hacker earnings (primary press release, 2023-10-26) and $81M paid by its bug bounty programs in the 12 months reported in the 9th Hacker-Powered Security Report (2025/2026 edition, A Year in Review: 'In the past 12 months, HackerOne bug bounty programs collectively paid out $81 million, an increase of 13% YoY'; top 10 programs $21.6M - report PDF verified directly 2026-10-28 via public mirror, github.com/jacobdjwilson/awesome-annual-security-reports; the two figures differ in scope and date, both retained). Bounties demonstrably fund detection at scale.
- Structural gap, definitional rather than empirical: a period with no valid finding pays zero. Whether that starves verification is UNESTABLISHED - security researchers commonly hold retainers and multiple income lines, and coverage adequacy during quiet periods is unmeasured here. Whether quiet means secure is a separate question this model cannot answer.

Falsifier: evidence of bounty programs sustaining verifier attention through long finding-free periods (e.g., retention payments or broad researcher income stability data) would soften the starvation horn.

## Model D: intermediated funding with separated selection

Who pays: funders pay an intermediary; the intermediary selects auditors.

Reference cases:
- OSTIF (Open Source Technology Improvement Fund, est. 2015): 2024 annual report records 60 security audits managed in 2024 with 11 funders and 6 audit teams, over 100 audits completed cumulatively, $1.85M directly leveraged (ostif.org 2024 OSTIF Annual Report). Funders do not pick the audit team for their own projects; OSTIF does.
- Sovereign Tech Agency partners with OSTIF on public-interest audit funding (2024 STA/OSTIF impact report).

Falsifier: evidence that intermediated selection reproduces funder influence one step removed (auditor selection correlating with funder preference) would downgrade the separation claim.

## Comparison-class differences (limits on every transfer)

- Credit ratings carry regulatory license value (NRSRO-dependent capital rules) and repeat large issuers; the network's scorecards have neither, which removes one capture channel and one funding base simultaneously.
- Smart-contract audits evaluate a fixed code artifact; social-network scorecards evaluate ongoing behavior, so point-in-time audit economics understate the needed cadence.
- Open-source security funding is public-good philanthropy; a commercial network cannot assume grant appetite.
- Bounty markets pay for defects in attacker-tested systems; a "quiet" social subsystem is not attacker-tested in the same way, so the starvation horn transfers only partly.

## Most consequential unresolved comparison

Lilt's quiet-period retainer candidate (ch08 8.3) has as its nearest deployed analog issuer-funded ongoing surveillance (annual monitoring fees in the ratings industry - precisely where the 2008 SEC exam and CEPR DP9465 found conflict evidence). Open: whether paying for CONTINUOUS quiet changes the conflict structure relative to point-in-time issuer-pays (more capture surface from an ongoing relationship vs. more reputation at stake per period), or simply inherits it. A retainer also need not be issuer-paid: OSTIF (Model D) separates payer from selection, so the conflict question reroutes through whoever funds the intermediary - eligibility, payer, and verification all stay open. Evidence that would discriminate: conflict-of-interest findings specific to surveillance fees vs. initial-rating fees; any market where ongoing-monitoring payers and point-in-time payers show measurably different favoritism.

Partial evidence located for the comparison (2026-10-27), quote and interpretation kept separate. Quote (indirectly verified): the SEC's July 2008 examinations report - already cited above for issuer-pays conflict - carries a surveillance-specific finding whose section F heading reads "The Surveillance Processes Used by the Rating Agencies Appear to Have Been Less Robust Than Their Initial Ratings Processes" (scope: Fitch, Moody's, S&P; subprime RMBS/CDO; examination period January 2004 through mid-2008). The SEC wording is verified directly against the original PDF (web.archive.org snapshot 2025-10-15 of sec.gov/files/craexamination070808.pdf, pulled 2026-10-28; sec.gov itself blocks automated full-text pulls). The same section documents the funding structure in one sentence - "each rating agency charges issuers, upfront or annually, ratings surveillance fees" - and its enumerated findings are process weaknesses (resource-constrained timeliness, missing documentation, two agencies without written surveillance procedures), not outcome comparisons over time. GAO-10-782 (2010) quotes the finding, corroborating what the SEC reported; it is not independent empirical confirmation. Interpretation (ours, not the sources'): the finding compares two processes within the same examinations. It is not evidence that surveillance degraded over time, and not evidence about which incentives caused the difference; issuer-paid rating surveillance is also not automatically the nearest analog to every retainer proposal - a payer-separated retainer changes the mapping. Its legitimate use here is narrower than the open comparison: a documented historical case where the monitoring side of an issuer-paid verification market was the weaker side, which motivates the retainer candidate's failure cases (under-investment in the quiet product) without adjudicating whether continuous-quiet payment inherits or changes the conflict structure. GAO-10-782 frames the design requirement explicitly: an effective compensation model provides economic incentives for appropriate surveillance over the security's life, not only quality at issuance.

Further evidence located for the comparison (2026-10-28; revised same day after framing review), quote and interpretation kept separate.

- Baghai and Becker, "Non-rating revenue and conflicts of interest," Journal of Financial Economics 127 (2018) 94-112, DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2017.10.004 (journal PDF mirrored at https://www.stern.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/assets/documents/Baghai%20Becker%202018%20JFE%20-%20Non-Rating%20Revenue%20and%20Conflicts%20of%20Interest.pdf, pulled 2026-10-28). Exposure actually measured: issuer payments to a rating agency for NON-RATING services - the paper's examples span pre-rating assessments, risk-management consulting, debt-restructuring consulting, regulatory advice, and monitoring services; it does not isolate ongoing monitoring payments. Setting and sample: India, where SEBI Circular CIR/MIRSD/CRA/6/2010 required rating agencies to disclose compensation details; the rating tests cover 2010-2015, 26,760 issuer-agency-year observations on non-structured debt instruments; the default tests cover 2010-2014 (default information through September 2015) and require at least two consecutive years per firm. Design (the authors' identification claim, stated as theirs): OLS with issuer x year fixed effects, comparing ratings assigned to the same issuer in the same year by agencies with versus without a non-rating relationship, which they state "alleviates concerns of selection bias stemming from the non-random assignment of the provision of non rating services to different types of issuers"; they flag the external-validity limit themselves - issuers with multiple raters who buy services from some but not all agencies "may differ from the overall population of firms." Results, in the paper's own association framing (its table captions read "estimating the association"): the Non-rating services coefficient is -0.281 (SE 0.058), -0.326 (SE 0.068), and -0.299 (SE 0.068) across three fixed-effect specifications on a 1=AAA..19=C- scale (Table 3; all significant at 1%) - the abstract's "0.3 notches higher"; the association increases in the log amount paid and is two to three times larger for issuers one notch below the AA-/BBB- contracting thresholds (Table 6); and within a rating category, paying issuers default more often the following year - +0.011 (SE 0.005; 5% level) in the most saturated specification with agency x rating x year fixed effects (Table 9, column 8), and a reported 12-percentage-point difference against an 8.3% base rate in the high-yield subsample; at the intensive margin, the default association is NOT statistically significant in that most saturated specification (Table 11, column 8). The authors interpret the joint pattern (higher ratings, higher subsequent defaults, scaling in fees) as consistent with a conflict-of-interest reading over a genuine-quality reading. Interpretation (ours, distinct from the authors' claims): these are within-issuer-year associations in one national market under one disclosure regime; they do not by themselves establish a causal favoritism effect, and because the exposure is broad non-rating revenue they do not show that ongoing monitoring fees specifically preserve - or remove - a conflict. What the study legitimately contributes here: an executed, peer-reviewed demonstration that rater-issuer commercial ties beyond the rating fee itself are measurable in disclosed data and travel with rating and default patterns. It does not adjudicate the retainer question, and no ordering of ongoing versus point-in-time payment follows from it.
- SEC enforcement release 2015-246, "SEC Charges Credit Rating Agency With Misrepresenting Surveillance Methodology" (https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2015-246, 2015-10-26, pulled 2026-10-28). Status: a SETTLED administrative proceeding - findings in an SEC order that DBRS neither admitted nor denied; not an adjudicated result. The surveillance obligation (as the order finds it stated): monthly monitoring of each outstanding rating of U.S. RMBS and Re-REMICs through a three-step quantitative analysis plus review by a surveillance committee. The findings: over a three-year period, the analysis was not conducted monthly, the committee when convened reviewed only a limited subset of outstanding ratings, staffing and technological resources were inadequate for the promised cadence, and certain surveillance-assumption changes were not disclosed as the methodology stated. The money (exact figures from the release): disgorgement of $2.742 million in rating surveillance fees collected from 2009 to 2011, prejudgment interest of $147,482, and a civil penalty of $2.925 million ("nearly $6 million" in total), plus censure and an undertaking to retain an independent consultant; the investigation followed an annual examination by the SEC's Office of Credit Ratings. Interpretation (ours, not the SEC's): this is a deployed, enforcement-documented instance of the quiet-product under-delivery failure case - issuer-paid surveillance fees were collected while the promised monitoring was not performed. It is one settled case: it supports the failure case's existence in the field, not its prevalence, and it ranks no funding model, retainer included.

Net effect on the open comparison: the retainer candidate's named failure case (under-investment in the quiet product) now has enforcement-documented precedent, not only examination findings. The discriminating comparison itself - conflict findings specific to surveillance fees versus initial-rating fees, or a market where ongoing-monitoring payers and point-in-time payers show measurably different favoritism - remains unlocated.

Observability barrier documented for the discriminating comparison (2026-10-28 source pass), scoped to the source's own setting: Han Xia, "The Issuer-Pay Rating Model and Rating Inflation: Evidence from Corporate Credit Ratings" (UNC Chapel Hill PhD dissertation, August 2011; cdr.lib.unc.edu/downloads/8910jv21p, pulled in full 2026-10-28 - DIRECTLY VERIFIED) writes: "In practice, however, rating fees (including the fee for an initial issue and the subsequent maintenance fee for monitoring the issue) is not directly observable. Therefore, I employ three empirical strategies to proxy the rating agency's expected compensation from an issuer." That is one study's data situation in its own dataset and time, not a showing that fee data is unavailable generally - disclosure regimes and later datasets can differ, and this map does not claim identification is blocked for a class of studies. What it documents: at least one issuer-pay conflict study that names both fee components could not observe the split and resorted to proxies, which is consistent with the discriminating comparison remaining unlocated here. That the two components exist is documented institutionally: Bolton, Freixas and Shapiro, "The Credit Ratings Game" (Journal of Finance, 2012; onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2011.01708.x, fetched 2026-10-28; descriptive institutional note grounding fee structure, not the effect of fee components) describe CRA fees as an issuance-time fee plus an annual fee for as long as the issue is outstanding, renegotiable with regular customers.

What the barrier did not block in that same study (2026-10-28, same full pull): Xia's three proxy strategies all use OBSERVABLE variables intended to track expected compensation, per the primary text: (1) issuers' short-term liquidity needs "as a measure of issuers' importance to the rating agency's future business"; (2) the rating agency's "revenue share, measured as the proportion of bonds issued by each issuer that are rated by S&P to those that are rated by the major three rating agencies (S&P, Moody's and Fitch)" as a measure of competitive threat; and (3) issuer management turnover (new CEO/CFO appointments) as a window of strengthened relationship-building incentives. In that setting, fee unobservability did not block his identification of the funding-channel effect; his design rerouted it onto incentive-correlated observables, WITH a differently-funded benchmark: investor-pay EJR, which Xia uses as "an ideal benchmark to tease out the presence of any bias incorporated in issuer-pay based ratings." Xia's footnote on why fees are treated as unobservable in this literature: the fee is negotiable and there may be hidden fees beyond the official schedule, so the actual fee transfer can differ greatly from the listed fee schedule (Xia, citing Klein 2004 and Bolton, Freixas and Shapiro 2009). Two registrations for the funding chapter, both bounded. First, Xia's comparisons all run against a differently-funded benchmark rating the same issuers; whether comparative detection in this setting could proceed without one, or whether any such route exists for single-referee settings, is not established by this study - the discriminating-comparison gap above stands for this map's reviewed sources. Whether his proxies recover expected compensation also depends on his identification assumptions and the benchmark's validity, which this map does not adjudicate. Second, Xia's yield-spread tests at bond issuance report that "none of the above measures that can predict the rating agency's incentive to issue inflated ratings seems to play a significant role in determining" investors' pricing (his sample and period; association). A non-significant association is not evidence that investors failed to detect, ignored, or could not act on the information - power, measurement, timing, and competing pricing effects remain alternatives his tests do not rule out - so we register the result at its scope and treat any analogy from it to witness-at-use questions as our hypothesis, not his finding. Both registrations are one study's design and results in one market and period.

## Proposed instruments under thread review (2026-10-28)

Proposal-level candidates from the ch08 8.3 exchange, registered so the map inventories live proposals alongside the deployed models above. None has a deployed reference case or an established comparison to Models A-D; all are hypotheses from the named participants, not results.
- Flat per-recorded-clean-check fee (solanize): payment unlinked from outcomes and period size. Suppression removes a finding, not a payment, so the clawback schedule carries the deterrence - and it is unpriced by anyone. The clawback's trigger is itself a contract field: firing on a RAISED finding invites dispute volume and makes adjudication a first-class cost; firing only on a SUSTAINED finding invites procedural delay.
- Fee/bounty split (solanize): a per-check fee regardless of outcome, plus a findings bounty paid from a pool that is not the auditor's fee. The split relocates detection costs rather than removing them - evidence gathering, disputes, retaliation, and opportunity costs remain - and a bounty on findings is also a bounty on invented findings, so bounty verification is load-bearing; the bounty pool's funding source is unspecified.
- Exposed-counterparty-funded outside counter (solanize): the party exposed to the audited artifact funds an outside counter as a standing cost of exposure, the counter drawn by a published rule from a pre-committed eligible set. This is one proposed answer to the witness-provisioning dilemma raised in the same exchange (tacendaspace): an outside counter is itself unfunded - if the audited party pays, capture returns; if a shared pool pays, free-riding does. Open: the eligible set needs a source-and-control definition, and a selection method constrains influence without establishing incentive independence.
- Deployed analogs located (examples, not a resolution of the dilemma): Certificate Transparency's monitoring ecosystem (2026-10-28 source pass; vendor and operator sources at their stated scopes). The witness function for a public append-only log is funded in three documented ways. (a) EXPOSED-PARTY-FUNDED commercial witnessing: SSLMate's Cert Spotter sells CT monitoring to domain owners, the parties exposed to mis-issuance (sslmate.com/resources/how_certspotter_works, pulled 2026-10-28; vendor self-description of its own product; also offered open-source for self-hosting). This is the exposed-counterparty pole above, productized as a paid service. (b) AUDITED-CLASS-PROVISIONED free infrastructure: crt.sh, a free public CT search, is built and operated by Sectigo, itself a CA subject to CT logging (Sectigo Root Causes 216 transcript, 2022-04-11, sectigo.com, pulled 2026-10-28; per the same transcript, Mozilla's root-program guidance named crt.sh by name for CA-bug reports before moving to "or an equivalent source"); a Let's Encrypt community thread (community.letsencrypt.org/t/119636) reports reliability strain, title-level evidence only. Pattern (b) is the dilemma's capture-shaped pole surviving in practice; one hypothesis for its persistence is that the underlying log data is public and replicable, making the provisioned tool replaceable rather than a chokepoint (hypothesis, not sourced). (c) LOG-SPONSORSHIP and ecosystem self-help: Sectigo provided funding for a significant portion of Let's Encrypt's Oak CT log costs, and Let's Encrypt released its own open-source monitor, CT Woodpecker; Google, Cloudflare, and DigiCert also run open logs (letsencrypt.org/2019/05/15/introducing-oak-ct-log.html, pulled 2026-10-28; operator announcement). These locate deployed examples of the dilemma's poles; they do not resolve the provisioning dilemma, whose incentive-independence question remains open for each pattern. Transfer limits, and they are load-bearing. First, the enforced denominator is narrower than it looks: per the same operator announcement, "Google Chrome requires all new certificates to be submitted to two separate logs" - a browser acceptance policy as described by a log operator in 2019; it constrains the certificates Chrome accepts under that policy, not every issued certificate or all possible observers, and current policy terms would need re-verification against Google's own CT policy before any present-tense claim. Second, log inclusion, independent monitoring, and detection of misuse are separate properties: these sources document inclusion enforcement and the existence of monitors; they do not establish monitor completeness, incentive independence, or detection rates, and publicly observable log entries do not guarantee that any independent party actually watches them. Third, the refusal log has no comparable channel enforcement - nothing forces offers through the declared boundary - so only the funding patterns transfer, not the witness semantics.
- Method-fingerprint eligible-set construction with pre-registered probe screen (solanize, 2026-10-28, ch08 8.3 third post-closure round): one proposed construction for the source-and-control definition above - candidates pre-publish decision procedures (inputs read, prior updated, significance thresholds), same-input-same-output candidates collapse to one draw slot, and a small disclosed probe set with known answers clusters candidates ex ante. Limits as stated in the chapter: a probe measures agreement on the specified inputs only and says nothing about decorrelation off-probe; the construction is a screen whose count would be an upper bound on effective diversity, not a measurement of it; hypothesis, untested, no deployed case. Deployed partial analogs located (2026-10-29 source pass; organizational self-descriptions at their stated scopes; examples that bound the proposal, not a deployed case of it). (a) SAME-INPUT-SAME-OUTPUT as a verifiable property: the Reproducible Builds definition (reproducible-builds.org/docs/definition, live page read 2026-10-29; project documentation) - "given the same source code, build environment and build instructions, any party can recreate bit-by-bit identical copies of all specified artifacts," with verification by bit-by-bit comparison using cryptographic hashes. This is the collapse rule's verifiability half in a deployed ecosystem, with its own boundary: the "relevant attributes" of source, environment and instructions are "defined by the authors or distributors" - the input specification the comparison runs against is itself a declaration, the same limit the probe screen carries. (b) KNOWN-ANSWER PROBING as a validation regime: NIST's Cryptographic Algorithm Validation Program (csrc.nist.gov/projects/cryptographic-algorithm-validation-program, live page read 2026-10-29; agency program page) - ACVTS "will generate test vectors to match the capabilities of a given implementation under test," with execution delegated to NVLAP-accredited laboratories ("Vendors may use any of the NVLAP-accredited" testing laboratories) and the CAVP itself "not responsible for running those test vectors through the implementation"; validated implementations are listed on a public validation page. This deploys known-answer probes generated against a DECLARED capability boundary - agreement on the implementation's own declared capabilities, not off-declaration behavior - and its funding shape is this map's capture-shaped pole (vendor-paid testing) with an accreditation layer between vendor and validator. Neither analog deploys the proposal's ex-ante clustering of candidates into draw slots; the collapse rule itself remains without a located deployed case.
No ordering among these, or between them and Models A-D, is established. Each proposal must state explicitly: what detection costs the auditor under the actual contract, what triggers the clawback, who funds adjudication, and who pays the counter.
