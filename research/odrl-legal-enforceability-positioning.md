# Legal-enforceability positioning for machine-readable usage terms (bounded pass, 2026-10-30; corrected 2026-10-30 per review)

Status: author research pass, one bounded round, primary/owning sources where verified, secondary commentary labeled as such. This is research positioning, not legal advice and not a legal conclusion. It records sourced questions and scoped holdings only; every unresolved interpretation is named. Scope question (from 7.5, claim 3.3): does anything in the located legal record bear on whether machine-readable usage terms attached to shared-private social edges could acquire legal force, and on the revocation-of-record semantics the consent-state machine specifies?

## Leg 1 - technical expression: what the ODRL specification says about itself
W3C's ODRL 2.2 Implementation Best Practices (https://w3c.github.io/poe/bp/, read 2026-10-30): "Even if ODRL is not capable of accessing the subtleties of legislation, the actions that are forbidden in a certain sorts of noms can be represented in ODRL and act as a summary." The same document lists summarization and compliance checking as the advantages of an ODRL representation. Scoped reading: the specification's own guidance describes ODRL's EXPRESSION limits. It does not adjudicate legal status - what a court would do with an ODRL policy is a separate question this document does not answer and does not claim to answer.

## Leg 2 - enforceability precedent: one copyright-license holding, scoped
Jacobsen v. Katzer, U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, 2008, appeal 2008-1001 (official precedential opinion: https://cafc.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/opinions-orders/08-1001.pdf; court case page: https://www.cafc.uscourts.gov/8-13-2008-2008-1001-jacobsen-v-katzer-08-1001/): the court's own framing - "We consider here the ability of a copyright holder to dedicate certain work to free public use and yet enforce an 'open source' copyright license to control the future distribution and modification of that work." The holding concerned the Artistic License in that dispute and treated its conditions as enforceable under copyright law rather than only as contractual covenants. Scope limits, stated explicitly: this is one U.S. appellate holding about a particular license and a particular copyright dispute; it does not establish anything about standardized machine-readable licenses generally, and it does not establish that copyright is the only possible enforceability route, in any jurisdiction. Creative Commons maintains a multi-jurisdiction case/scholarship database (https://legaldb.creativecommons.org/, read 2026-10-30) whose own disclaimer states it "is not comprehensive, and CC does not necessarily endorse the views of the courts or scholars." This bounded pass located no authority applying an enforceability mechanism to usage terms over social-graph edge facts - a bounded-search result, not a legal characterization: contractual, database-right, privacy-statute and other routes were not ruled out and were not all researched (see Leg 4).

## Leg 3 - revocation duties in EU data-protection law (provisions separated, holding scoped)
Distinct provisions, not one doctrine (texts via the CJEU judgment's own recitation, below, and EUR-Lex 32016R0679): withdrawal of consent (Art. 7(3)), rectification (Art. 16), erasure (Art. 17(1)), restriction (Art. 18), and the recipient-notification obligation (Art. 19). Article 19 as quoted verbatim in the Proximus judgment (para. 15): "The controller shall communicate any rectification or erasure of personal data or restriction of processing carried out in accordance with Article 16, Article 17(1) and Article 18 to each recipient to whom the personal data have been disclosed, unless this proves impossible or involves disproportionate effort. The controller shall inform the data subject about those recipients if the data subject requests it."
CJEU, Case C-129/21 Proximus (judgment 27 Oct 2022, Belgian directory-services reference; official text EUR-Lex CELEX 62021CJ0129): verified from the judgment text - a subscriber's request to withdraw personal data from directories was held to constitute making use of the right to erasure within the meaning of Article 17 (answer to the second question, para. 71); the third question asked whether Articles 5(2) and 24 GDPR (accountability; controller responsibility) permit a supervisory authority to require a directory provider, as controller, to take appropriate technical and organisational measures to inform third-party controllers - the supplying operator and the other directory providers it supplied - of the subscriber's consent withdrawal (para. 72, framing verified). Verified verbatim from the official judgment text (read 2026-10-30): the answer to the third question is that "Article 5(2) and Article 24 of the GDPR must be interpreted as meaning that a national supervisory authority may require that the provider of directories, as controller, take appropriate technical and organisational measures to inform third-party controllers, namely the telephone service operator which has communicated its subscriber's personal data to that provider and the other providers of directories to which that provider has itself supplied such data, of the withdrawal of the subscriber's consent"; and the answer to the fourth question is that "Article 17(2) of the GDPR must be interpreted as not precluding a national supervisory authority from ordering a provider of directories - which has been requested by the subscriber of a telephone service operator to cease disclosing personal data relating to him or her - to take 'reasonable steps', within the meaning of that provision, to inform search engine providers of that request for erasure of the data" (Article 17(2)'s own limit is "taking account of available technology and the cost of implementation"). Two regulator actions verified from the agencies' own press releases, recorded as specific enforcement facts, not a general rule about notification: Spain's AEPD, 18 May 2022, fined Google EUR 10 million for transferring erasure-request data (requester identity, email, reasons, URL) to the Lumen Project without a legal basis and for hindering the right to erasure, Articles 6 and 17 GDPR (https://www.aepd.es/en/prensa-y-comunicacion/notas-de-prensa/the-aepd-has-imposed-sanction-on-google-llc-for-transferring-personal-data-to-third-parties); Sweden's IMY, 11 March 2020, fined Google SEK 75 million for non-compliance with delisting obligations, including an order to cease informing site-owners when listings are removed, stating Google "does not have a legal basis for informing site-owners when search result listings are removed" (https://www.imy.se/en/about-us/arkiv/nyhetsarkiv/the-swedish-data-protection-authority-imposes-administrative-fine-on-google/; appeal status after the release's three-week window is not verified here; a commentary figure of SEK 50 million is superseded by the agency's own release).
Explicit non-claims (corrected per review): nothing located establishes that the statutory "impossible or involves disproportionate effort" exception corresponds to a technical partition condition in a distributed system, or that a distributed-system limit would constitute legal excuse; that mapping is an open interpretation question, not a conclusion. And the Article 19 duty runs between controllers and recipients under the GDPR's definitions - nothing located extends it to peer-to-peer shared-private edges between private parties outside GDPR scope.
