# Legal-enforceability positioning for machine-readable usage terms (bounded pass, 2026-10-30)

Status: author research pass, one bounded round, four legs, primary/owning sources where available. This is research positioning, not legal advice and not a legal conclusion. Facts, what-they-don't-establish, and the residual are separated. Scope question (from 7.5, claim 3.3): does anything in the located legal record bear on whether machine-readable usage terms attached to shared-private social edges could acquire legal force, and on the revocation-of-record semantics the consent-state machine specifies?

## Leg 1 - technical expression: what ODRL says it is (owning source)
W3C's ODRL 2.2 Implementation Best Practices (w3c.github.io/poe/bp/, read 2026-10-30): "Even if ODRL is not capable of accessing the subtleties of legislation, the actions that are forbidden in a certain sorts of noms can be represented in ODRL and act as a summary." The same document lists the advantages of an ODRL representation as summarization and compliance checking. The owning source positions ODRL as an expression and summarization layer with compliance-checking utility, explicitly not a carrier of legislative effect. What this does not establish: that an ODRL policy is legally inert in every context - it establishes what the specification claims for itself, not what a court would do with it.

## Leg 2 - enforceability across jurisdictions: the Creative Commons record and its mechanism
The closest enforceability precedent class for standardized machine-readable rights expressions is the Creative Commons license suite. CC maintains a multi-jurisdiction Legal Database of cases and scholarship (legaldb.creativecommons.org, read 2026-10-30), with CC's own disclaimer that the database "is not comprehensive, and CC does not necessarily endorse the views of the courts or scholars." The leading US authority, Jacobsen v. Katzer (Federal Circuit, 2008, cafc.uscourts.gov), held that an open-source copyright license's conditions are enforceable as copyright conditions, not merely contractual covenants - i.e., enforceability runs through the copyright regime over copies. What this does not establish: any transfer to social-graph usage terms. CC's enforceability hook is copyright in the licensed work; a social-graph edge (a fact of authorship, membership, or reference between parties) is not a copyrighted work in the same way, and nothing located extends the CC mechanism to usage terms over such facts. The mechanism matters, not the headline: "standardized machine-readable licenses can be enforceable" is established for the copyright-hook case only.

## Leg 3 - revocation: the statutory downstream-propagation duty (GDPR Art. 19 + Proximus)
The revocation leg has a direct statutory analog. GDPR Article 19 (text via gdpr-law.eu, read 2026-10-30): the controller shall communicate any rectification, erasure (Art. 17(1)) or restriction (Art. 18) "to each recipient to whom the personal data have been disclosed, unless this proves impossible or involves disproportionate effort," and shall inform the data subject about those recipients on request. The CJEU in Proximus (C-129/21, 27 Oct 2022) read Art. 19 with the accountability principle to require a directory provider to pass a consent withdrawal on to the other directory providers it supplied. Supervisory practice adds both edges of the duty: Sweden reprimanded Klarna for failing to tell a customer which recipients received his data (with the burden of showing impossibility/disproportionate effort placed on the controller), while Spain (AEPD, EUR 10,000,000) and Sweden (IMY, SEK 50,000,000 final) fined Google for the reverse problem - forwarding erasure-request details to parties the law did not require, notification being processing in its own right. What this establishes for the paper: a statute already imposes a downstream-propagation duty on withdrawal/erasure for personal data, scoped to recipients of the disclosure, with the law's own answer to the partition/observability problem the consent-state machine names - propagation is excused when impossible or disproportionate, and the burden of documenting that sits with the propagator. What this does not establish: that any analogous duty attaches to the paper's shared-private edges between private parties outside GDPR scope, or that a receipt-layer propagation mechanism satisfies any legal duty; the Art. 19 duty runs controller-to-recipients under the GDPR's definitions, not peer-to-peer.

## Leg 4 - contractual assent: NOT COVERED
Whether machine-readable terms could acquire contractual force through assent mechanics (clickwrap/browsewrap analogies) is named and deliberately unresearched in this pass (stopping rule: one round). It is a distinct doctrine and a shallow treatment would mislead.

## Residual (named, not resolved)
The 3.3 legal gap narrows but stays open. What the located record positions: (a) the expression layer is honest about its own limits (ODRL's own text); (b) enforceability of standardized machine-readable terms exists in the copyright-hook class and nothing located transfers it to social-edge facts; (c) revocation-of-record has a statutory analog with a documented impossibility/disproportionate-effort boundary - the same boundary the machine's partition analysis names - plus an over-notification counter-duty that maps to the paper's own minimization constraints. What remains a legal-expertise question: whether any enforceability hook (copyright, contract, data-protection statute) could attach to the paper's specific shared-private usage terms, in any jurisdiction. That question still wants a qualified reader; this pass does not answer it and does not pretend to.
