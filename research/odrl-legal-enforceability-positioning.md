# Legal-enforceability positioning for machine-readable usage terms (bounded pass, 2026-10-30; corrected 2026-10-30 per review)

Status: author research pass, one bounded round, primary/owning sources where verified, secondary commentary labeled as such. This is research positioning, not legal advice and not a legal conclusion. It records sourced questions and scoped holdings only; every unresolved interpretation is named. Scope question (from 7.5, claim 3.3): does anything in the located legal record bear on whether machine-readable usage terms attached to shared-private social edges could acquire legal force, and on the revocation-of-record semantics the consent-state machine specifies?

## Leg 1 - technical expression: what the ODRL specification says about itself
W3C's ODRL 2.2 Implementation Best Practices (https://w3c.github.io/poe/bp/, read 2026-10-30): "Even if ODRL is not capable of accessing the subtleties of legislation, the actions that are forbidden in a certain sorts of noms can be represented in ODRL and act as a summary." The same document lists summarization and compliance checking as the advantages of an ODRL representation. Scoped reading: the specification's own guidance describes ODRL's EXPRESSION limits. It does not adjudicate legal status - what a court would do with an ODRL policy is a separate question this document does not answer and does not claim to answer.

## Leg 2 - enforceability precedent: one copyright-license holding, scoped
Jacobsen v. Katzer, U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, 2008 (opinion at cafc.uscourts.gov, case 2008-1001): the court's own framing - "We consider here the ability of a copyright holder to dedicate certain work to free public use and yet enforce an 'open source' copyright license to control the future distribution and modification of that work." The holding concerned the Artistic License in that dispute and treated its conditions as enforceable under copyright law rather than only as contractual covenants. Scope limits, stated explicitly: this is one U.S. appellate holding about a particular license and a particular copyright dispute; it does not establish anything about standardized machine-readable licenses generally, and it does not establish that copyright is the only possible enforceability route, in any jurisdiction. Creative Commons maintains a multi-jurisdiction case/scholarship database (https://legaldb.creativecommons.org/, read 2026-10-30) whose own disclaimer states it "is not comprehensive, and CC does not necessarily endorse the views of the courts or scholars." This bounded pass located no authority applying an enforceability mechanism to usage terms over social-graph edge facts - a bounded-search result, not a legal characterization: contractual, database-right, privacy-statute and other routes were not ruled out and were not all researched (see Leg 4).

## Leg 3 - revocation duties in EU data-protection law (provisions separated, holding scoped)
Distinct provisions, not one doctrine (texts via the CJEU judgment's own recitation, below, and EUR-Lex 32016R0679): withdrawal of consent (Art. 7(3)), rectification (Art. 16), erasure (Art. 17(1)), restriction (Art. 18), and the recipient-notification obligation (Art. 19). Article 19 as quoted verbatim in the Proximus judgment (para. 15): "The controller shall communicate any rectification or erasure of personal data or restriction of processing carried out in accordance with Article 16, Article 17(1) and Article 18 to each recipient to whom the personal data have been disclosed, unless this proves impossible or involves disproportionate effort. The controller shall inform the data subject about those recipients if the data subject requests it."
CJEU, Case C-129/21 Proximus (judgment 27 Oct 2022, Belgian directory-services reference; official text EUR-Lex CELEX 62021CJ0129): verified from the judgment text - a subscriber's request to withdraw personal data from directories was held to constitute making use of the right to erasure within the meaning of Article 17 (answer to the second question, para. 71); the third question asked whether Articles 5(2) and 24 GDPR (accountability; controller responsibility) permit a supervisory authority to require a directory provider, as controller, to take appropriate technical and organisational measures to inform third-party controllers - the supplying operator and the other directory providers it supplied - of the subscriber's consent withdrawal (para. 72, framing verified). The answer to the third question is reported by secondary commentary (gdpr-law.eu's Article 19 commentary, read 2026-10-30) as requiring the provider to pass the withdrawal on to the other providers it supplied and, in the circumstances of that case, to the supplying operator; the full operative paragraph was not re-verified in the official text in this pass (fetch truncation) and is marked commentary-sourced. Separately documented regulator actions reported by the same commentary: Spain's AEPD fined Google EUR 10,000,000 (2022) for forwarding erasure-request details to the Lumen database without a legal basis, and Sweden's IMY fined Google SEK 50,000,000 (final, 2020) over informing site owners of delistings. These are two specific enforcement facts, not a general rule about notification.
Explicit non-claims (corrected per review): nothing located establishes that the statutory "impossible or involves disproportionate effort" exception corresponds to a technical partition condition in a distributed system, or that a distributed-system limit would constitute legal excuse; that mapping is an open interpretation question, not a conclusion. And the Article 19 duty runs between controllers and recipients under the GDPR's definitions - nothing located extends it to peer-to-peer shared-private edges between private parties outside GDPR scope.
